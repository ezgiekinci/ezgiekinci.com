Inputs & Retention Model

Each game came with three retention points, a CPI, an organic rate (extra organic installs per paid install) and ARPDAU. To estimate retention on every day up to 180, I fitted a power-law curve through the three points with a log-log regression.

Retention curve

R(t) = a · t−b

b measures how fast players leave; a anchors Day 1.

6-month lifetime

LT180 = 1 + Σ R(t), t = 1…179

Active days an average install generates in 180 days, counting install day as one.

Effective LTV

LTVeff = LT180 × ARPDAU × (1 + organic)

Each paid install also brings organic installs, so they count toward its value.

Which Game Earns the Most at 6 Months?

To compare at equal spend, I put the same $10,000 UA budget into each game. Lower CPI means more installs for the same money, so I compared total net profit rather than profit per install.

Winner: Game C. Game A keeps players longest (highest LT180, best D30), but its $4.00 CPI caps volume at 2,500 installs. Game C’s $2.50 CPI buys 4,000 installs, and with the best D1 (50%) and highest ARPDAU ($0.35) it ends $7,867 ahead of Game A. Game D has the second-longest lifetime, but low ARPDAU and organic rate leave it with the lowest effective LTV. All four games pay back within six months; the question is where each dollar works hardest.

03

When Does a $1k/Day Campaign Break Even?

Spending $1,000 a day on Game A from 1 September 2026 buys 250 paid installs a day (437.5 with organics). Every day a new cohort joins and older cohorts keep playing, so daily revenue on day D is effective users × ARPDAU × cumulative retention up to age D−1.

Which Ad Should Scale? Three Wonder Link Creatives

A stronger hook raises click-through, and ad platforms reward that with cheaper impressions, so CPI falls. But an ad also sets expectations: if the game isn’t what the ad showed, players uninstall and the cheap install was never cheap. I judged each ad on both sides.

Recommendation: scale Ad 2. Its fail-bait hook (“I would never make that mistake”) should out-click Ad 1’s passive positive hook, and it’s the shortest ad with the only animated CTA. Unlike Ad 3, it shows the real game, so the players it brings in find what they expected and stay. At scale, what matters is spend per retained user, and Ad 3’s low CPI is wiped out by early churn. These are directional estimates from the creatives themselves; I’d confirm them with a test.

05

Two Ad Concepts to Lower CPI for What The Hex

Before writing concepts, I studied the ads of five leading hex-sort competitors. The winners share three things: they open on a fully packed board, use a tutorial hand to guide (or misguide) the action, and show locked sections to spark curiosity about what comes next.

Concept 1 · Positive hook

The “Satisfying ASMR” Cascade

Open on a chaotic, nearly full board. The hand drops one crucial stack into a last empty slot, a crisp click sounds, and a chain reaction merges colour after colour until the board clears. The camera then moves to padlocked areas and a pulsing “PLAY NOW”.

Why it lowers CPI: near-failure tension makes the release more rewarding, and the locks end on curiosity.

Concept 2 · Reactance hook

The “Obvious Mistake” Fail Bait

“Only 5% can unlock this!” over a full board. The hand makes two good moves to teach the mechanic, then hesitates at the obvious winning move, picks the wrong stack and fails. A buzzer, a grey flash, a “FAIL” stamp, then “Think you can unlock it?” with the hand tapping PLAY.

Why it lowers CPI: people click to prove they’d do better, and higher CTR lowers the cost of every install.

I deliberately left the game’s town-building meta out of both concepts: in a 30-second ad, mixing meta progression with the core puzzle splits attention and weakens the hook.
