Match Squad: Reading 100 Levels & Designing a Villain

I played levels 1–100 on a fresh account and logged every one of them to reverse-engineer the obstacle system and its pacing, took a single level apart to reconstruct the designer’s thinking, and designed a new villain and obstacle for the game’s dice levels.

01

Method: A Log of Every Level

The brief asked three things: read the obstacle system across the first 100 levels, explain the reasoning behind one level, and design something new for the dice levels. Every number on this page comes from the spreadsheet I kept while playing.

Level log

For every level: objective, target counts, moves granted, obstacles present, attempts needed and moves left on the win.

Obstacle catalogue

Each obstacle’s mechanic and visual concept, plus my own 1–5 rating of whether the art teaches the rule before the tutorial does.

Honest limits

One player, outside-in, no internal telemetry. Attempt counts describe my experience, not the player base. The recording for level 97 was lost, so only its attempt count survives.

Where it works least well: Soil is the only obstacle several people I asked could not identify at all (the most common guess was clay), and I only understood it by trial. Border attacks the player’s information rather than space, an interesting idea, but after twenty levels I still could not reconcile the goal counter with what I could see on the board. Difficulty that comes from opacity is different from difficulty that comes from the puzzle.

03

The Decade Is the Unit of Design

From level 21 onward the game runs the same ten-level template, without a single exception across all eight remaining decades. New ordinary obstacles arrive exactly on levels 21, 31, 41 … 91.

PositionLevel typeDice bonusFunction
X1Normal–New obstacle, alone on the board
X2Normal–New obstacle plus exactly one known obstacle
X3Normal–A third obstacle enters
X4Hard×2The new mechanic at full complexity
X5Dice Clash×5Boss
X6–X8Normal–Older obstacles rotate; the new one returns at X6 and X8
X9Super Hard×3Second ramp
X0Super Clash×10Boss

Teach, test, forget, remember

A new obstacle appears on X1–X4 without a break, then returns at X6 and X8 with a two-level gap: just enough to forget and have to remember.

Variety from combination

A fixed template means designers only choose which obstacles go in a level. Eighteen obstacles produce a hundred essentially unique levels.

20 boss fights from 4 assets

Boss obstacles arrive at levels 5, 15, 35 and 75; the other sixteen Clash levels remix them. The boss is an exam on everything learned so far.

This also explains why the brief excluded levels ending in 1 or 2 from the deep dive: X1 teaches and X2 rehearses, so both are tutorial levels.

Key Insight: Attempts roughly double while the move budget falls by four. The ramp doesn’t come from harder obstacles but from the same obstacles with fewer moves. And the labels don’t match the experience: “Hard” is statistically indistinguishable from Normal (1.62 vs 1.45 attempts, identical 75% first-try rate) while paying a ×2 reward. Super Hard is the real wall.

05

Anatomy of a Single Level: Level 54

Level 54 sits in the X4 “Hard” slot, whose job is to test the decade’s new obstacle (Crystals) at full complexity. It uses four obstacles stacked in layers rather than side by side: Ivy on the surface, Sapphire columns in the middle, Crystal cabinets to the right and Ice underneath. Clearing each one grants access to the next, so four chores become a single chain.

16 / 82

Cells playable at move one

And they sit in the top-right corner, the point furthest from every goal. The level opens by telling you there’s no room to pursue anything yet.

1 route

The dead zone enforces order

An empty rectangle bends the board into a C and cuts off the cabinets from above. Without it there would be two routes; with it, the solve order is singular.

56%

Of the goal falls in the last 4 moves

The ice counter sat at 16 for 24 of my 26 moves. Four-fifths of the level is excavation with almost nothing to show, then an avalanche.

What I think is strong

  • The board writes its own solution order: ivy, sapphire, crystals, ice, read top to bottom around the C.
  • The hardest constraint (colour-matching beside a specific cabinet) arrives last, when the board is most open and special pieces are plentiful.

What I think is weak

  • Variance decided the ending: an unplanned Powerball cascade dropped crystals from 10 to 2 in one move. I cleared it first try with 9 moves spare and no boosters, and I’m not confident that was repeatable.
  • The “Hard” label doesn’t mean much here or anywhere else in the first 100 levels.

What I think the designer was solving: test Crystals without turning them into a colour-matching drill. Four cabinets in the open would reduce to “make green matches next to the blue box” sixteen times. Burying them turns a repetitive requirement into a paced spatial problem. The ice underneath isn’t a fourth objective so much as a receipt: it can only be completed if the other three were done in the right order.

06

A New Villain: TEMPEST & the BOLT Obstacle

The gap: All four existing bosses attack either the number of goals or the player’s resources. Nothing attacks the board. Every one of the 18 obstacles acts inside its own cell. TEMPEST attacks the player’s information about their next move.

TEMPEST, the villain

An old pig in a white-and-gold wizard’s robe who stirs the board every 5 moves, reshuffling loose tiles and dropping 3 new BOLTs. The orb on his staff lights up one move before each storm, so the warning lives on the character.

BOLT, the obstacle

A lightning bolt inside a storm cloud. One adjacent match breaks it: no layers, no colour rule, the most familiar verb in the game. Like Sphere at level 5, the object is easy on purpose, so the only new thing to learn is the storm.

Five rules so the stir reads as “adapt”, not “bad luck”

Tile restrictionOnly loose coloured tiles shuffle; obstacles and boosters stay put, or a layered board becomes unreadable.
Fair board stateA stir can never produce free matches. Otherwise the boss would sometimes hand out gifts.
One-move warningThe staff glows on the 4th move. You can’t adapt to what you can’t see; the warning turns randomness into a deadline.
BOLT drop rules3 BOLTs land after each shuffle, spaced apart, in visible spots. No clustering.
CapAbove a ceiling the storm still comes but drops nothing. The exact number is a tuning question I’d leave to live data.

The tutorial level

It reuses Level 5’s outline but inverts the field: 16 BOLTs instead of 34 Spheres, so 33 of 49 cells are free instead of 15, because open space is the material a stir works on. Goal: 20 BOLTs in 25 moves, no other obstacles. The storm counter only starts after the first moves have taught what a BOLT is.

What I’m not sure about

The right cap. Whether a stir belongs on layered boards at all (I’d keep TEMPEST off Ice, Crystals and Sapphire levels). And fatigue: the stir is the biggest visual event in the game, and a player who stops reading a board that keeps being rewritten is the failure mode.

Beyond the Case: Economy & Monetization Teardown

Alongside the case I wrote an outside-in product teardown from playing through level 374, covering the core loop, the dice economy and the offer ladder. A few findings:

77%

Of dice come from 4 levels in 10

20 of the 26 dice a cycle pays come from the multiplier levels, the ones most likely to make you fail. Difficulty spike, dice payday and the 900-coin continue offer are the same moment.

900 / 1,000

Continue vs. cheapest coin pack

The ₺99.99 pack buys exactly one continue and strands 100 coins. At ~30 coins per level, one continue is about 30 levels of grinding.

~8×

Dice Rain vs. shop dice

The shop sells dice at ₺3.33 each; the triggered Dice Rain offer at ₺0.43. The standing pack exists mainly as the anchor that makes the offer look extraordinary.

Proposal: a bounded revenge loop

Players get attacked by real players but can’t strike back, so an attack feels like pure loss. A “recent attackers” list with one free counter-strike per attack, expiring in 24 hours, turns an anonymous loss into a named reason to open the notification. It reuses the existing attack minigame.

Metrics: sessions from attack notifications, revenge usage, D7. Guardrail: churn among frequently attacked low-level players.

Proposal: a procedurally generated second mode

Dice, events and streaks are all fed by hand-authored match-3 levels, the studio’s most expensive content. A sliding-block mode can be generated and difficulty-graded algorithmically, minting dice at a controlled rate and giving players who hit the content ceiling somewhere to go.

Metrics: share of dice from the new mode, ceiling-cohort retention, cost per retained day. Guardrail: time in the main track.
